Here's a deeper, more thorough explanation of the human circulatory system. I'll go from the blood itself, to vessels, to the heart, and finally to how circulation actually works — explaining not just *what* things are but *why* and *how* they function.

## What the Circulatory System Is and Why We Need It

Every cell in your body needs a constant supply of oxygen and nutrients and needs its wastes carried away. In a single-celled organism, materials just diffuse in and out. But in a large multicellular body, diffusion alone is far too slow to reach cells deep inside. So we evolved a **transport system** — a network of vessels, a pump (the heart), and a fluid (blood) — to move materials rapidly to and from every cell.

The system is technically called the **cardiovascular system** ("cardio" = heart, "vascular" = vessels). It performs three broad jobs: **transport** (oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, hormones, wastes), **protection** (immune cells and clotting), and **regulation** (of body temperature, pH, and water balance).

## Blood — Studied in Depth

Blood is classified as a **connective tissue** because its cells are suspended in a non-living fluid matrix (plasma). An adult has about 5 litres of it. When spun in a centrifuge, blood separates into layers that reveal its composition.

**Plasma (~55%)** is the straw-coloured fluid matrix. About 90–92% is water, and the rest is dissolved materials. The most important dissolved substances are the **plasma proteins**:
- *Albumin* maintains **osmotic pressure**, which keeps fluid inside the blood vessels rather than leaking into tissues.
- *Globulins* include antibodies (immune defence) and transport proteins.
- *Fibrinogen* is essential for **clotting** — it converts to fibrin threads that trap cells and seal wounds.
Plasma also carries glucose, amino acids, hormones, mineral ions, and dissolved gases and wastes like urea.

**Red Blood Cells (Erythrocytes, ~5 million per cubic mm):** Their structure beautifully matches their function. They are **biconcave discs**, a shape that increases surface area for gas exchange and lets them squeeze through narrow capillaries. In mammals they **lack a nucleus and most organelles** when mature — this frees up internal space for more **hemoglobin** and means they don't consume the oxygen they carry. Hemoglobin is a protein with four iron-containing **heme** groups; each iron atom binds one oxygen molecule, forming bright-red **oxyhemoglobin**. When oxygen is released to tissues, the blood turns darker. RBCs are made in the **red bone marrow**, live about 120 days, and are then broken down in the spleen and liver.

**White Blood Cells (Leukocytes, ~5,000–10,000 per cubic mm):** These are the immune defenders. Unlike RBCs, they **have a nucleus** and can move out of blood vessels into tissues. They fall into two groups:
- *Granulocytes* (have granules): **Neutrophils** engulf bacteria (phagocytosis); **eosinophils** fight parasites and allergies; **basophils** release histamine in inflammation.
- *Agranulocytes*: **Lymphocytes** (B-cells make antibodies, T-cells destroy infected cells) and **monocytes** (mature into large phagocytes called macrophages).

**Platelets (Thrombocytes, ~250,000 per cubic mm):** Not whole cells but **fragments** of larger bone-marrow cells. When a vessel is injured, platelets stick to the wound, clump together, and trigger the **clotting cascade** in which fibrinogen becomes fibrin, forming a mesh that stops bleeding.

**Blood groups explained:** RBC surfaces carry marker molecules called **antigens**. In the **ABO system**, type A has A antigens, type B has B antigens, AB has both, and O has neither. Your plasma contains **antibodies** against the antigens you lack (e.g., type A blood has anti-B antibodies). This is why mismatched transfusions are dangerous — antibodies attack foreign RBCs, causing them to clump (agglutinate). Type **O⁻ is the universal donor** (no antigens to attack) and **AB⁺ is the universal recipient** (no antibodies to attack donor cells). The **Rh factor** is another antigen; someone who has it is Rh⁺, someone without it is Rh⁻. This matters especially in pregnancy, where an Rh⁻ mother carrying an Rh⁺ baby can develop antibodies against the baby's blood.

## Blood Vessels — Structure Matched to Function

All vessels except capillaries have three layers: an inner lining (**endothelium**), a middle layer of muscle and elastic tissue, and an outer connective tissue coat. The differences between vessel types come from how thick these layers are.

**Arteries** carry blood *away* from the heart. Because the heart pumps blood into them under high pressure, they have **thick, muscular, elastic walls** that stretch with each surge and recoil to keep blood moving. Their elasticity is why you can feel a **pulse**. They branch into smaller **arterioles**, which can constrict or dilate to control how much blood reaches an area.

**Capillaries** are the functional heart of the whole system — everything else exists to serve them. They are only **one cell thick**, so oxygen, glucose, and wastes can diffuse easily across their walls. They form dense networks (capillary beds) so that no cell is far from one. This is the only place where **exchange with tissues** actually occurs.

**Veins** carry blood *back* to the heart. By this point pressure is very low, so veins have **thinner walls and a wider lumen**. Their key feature is **one-way valves** that stop blood from flowing backward. Blood is pushed along partly by the squeezing of surrounding **skeletal muscles** (the "muscle pump") as you move.

## The Heart — In Detail

The heart is a fist-sized muscular pump made of a special involuntary tissue called **cardiac muscle (myocardium)**, which never fatigues. It sits in the chest, slightly left of centre, enclosed in a double protective membrane called the **pericardium** that contains lubricating fluid to reduce friction as it beats.

**Four chambers:** The two upper chambers are the **atria** (thin-walled, they *receive* blood) and the two lower chambers are the **ventricles** (thick-walled, they *pump* blood out). The **left ventricle** has by far the thickest wall because it must generate enough force to push blood around the *entire* body, whereas the right ventricle only pumps to the nearby lungs.

**The septum** is the solid muscular wall dividing the heart's left and right sides. Its job is crucial: it **prevents oxygenated and deoxygenated blood from mixing**, which keeps oxygen delivery efficient.

**The valves** ensure blood flows in only one direction:
- The **atrioventricular (AV) valves** sit between atria and ventricles: the **tricuspid** (right side, three flaps) and the **bicuspid/mitral** (left side, two flaps). They're anchored by tendon-like cords (*chordae tendineae*) so they don't turn inside out under pressure.
- The **semilunar valves** (aortic and pulmonary) guard the exits into the great arteries, preventing blood from flowing back into the ventricles.
The classic "**lub-dub**" heart sound is these valves snapping shut — "lub" is the AV valves closing, "dub" is the semilunar valves closing.

**How the heartbeat is generated (the heart is *myogenic*):** The heart creates its own electrical signal. A patch of tissue in the right atrium called the **Sino-Atrial (SA) node** acts as the natural **pacemaker**, firing impulses that make the atria contract. The signal reaches the **Atrio-Ventricular (AV) node**, which delays it briefly (so atria empty fully), then races down fibres (the **bundle of His** and **Purkinje fibres**) to make the ventricles contract from the bottom up. This electrical activity is what an **ECG** records.

**The cardiac cycle** is one complete heartbeat, split into **systole** (contraction, blood pushed out) and **diastole** (relaxation, chambers refill). At rest this repeats about **70–72 times a minute**. **Blood pressure** (normally ~120/80 mmHg) measures the force on artery walls during systole (120) and diastole (80).

## Circulation — How Blood Actually Moves

Humans have a **double circulation**, meaning blood passes through the heart **twice** during one complete trip around the body. This was famously worked out by **William Harvey**. The advantage is that blood can be re-pressurised by the heart before being sent to the body, allowing fast, high-pressure delivery while keeping oxygen-rich and oxygen-poor blood completely separate.

**Loop 1 — Pulmonary circulation (heart → lungs → heart):**
Deoxygenated blood leaves the **right ventricle** through the **pulmonary artery** and travels to the lungs. In the lung capillaries around the air sacs (alveoli), it **releases carbon dioxide and absorbs oxygen**. Now oxygen-rich, it returns via the **pulmonary veins** to the **left atrium**. (Note the exception: here the artery carries deoxygenated blood and the vein carries oxygenated blood — opposite of the usual rule.)

**Loop 2 — Systemic circulation (heart → body → heart):**
Oxygenated blood leaves the **left ventricle** through the **aorta**, the body's largest artery, which branches to supply every organ and tissue. In the tissue capillaries, oxygen and nutrients are delivered and carbon dioxide and wastes are collected. The now-deoxygenated blood returns through veins that merge into the **superior and inferior vena cava**, emptying into the **right atrium** to begin the cycle again.

**The complete pathway in sequence:**
Body tissues → vena cava → **right atrium** → tricuspid valve → **right ventricle** → pulmonary artery → **lungs** → pulmonary vein → **left atrium** → bicuspid valve → **left ventricle** → aorta → **body tissues** → (repeat).

**Two special circulations worth knowing:**
- **Coronary circulation** supplies the heart *muscle itself* with blood through the coronary arteries. A blockage here starves the muscle and causes a **heart attack**.
- The **hepatic portal system** is unusual: blood carrying absorbed nutrients from the intestines goes first to the **liver** (via the hepatic portal vein) for processing and detoxification *before* returning to the heart.
